Workshop of Why

A workshop story about light and checking AI suggestions.

Ages 7–13 · one short investigation · no account

What the child does

Move Tavi the paper bird by dragging, tapping the scene, clicking an object label or using arrow keys. The window, mirror and prism supply clues.

Mira asks small questions. A mistaken or uncertain reply opens a simpler question; a correct reply leads directly onward. No separate next button is needed.

Optional practice AI

The child tries an idea first. They may then ask the practice AI. The reply button announces its effort cost: one extra evidence-check question. There is no help quota. Ordinary guidance and exploring remain free.

Some AI responses are false, some useful but incomplete, and others adequate. Mira models checking the first two requested suggestions. The child must answer the check; merely flagging a line earns no checking credit.

What it practises

A plausible answer can need more detail. Compare it with an observation, identify what is missing, and make your own judgement. Successful checks earn recognition.

Playing together

Read each reply slowly. Ask what the child observed before naming the answer. There is no timer, score penalty or need to look at the Sun.

Science and limits

This is an authored story, not a real AI chat or physical simulation. The diagrams compare blue with red light; ray counts are not measurements. A prism separates existing colours; air scatters light. No personal information is collected.

An ordinary mirror has a thin silver or aluminium coating. Light drives mobile electrons in the metal, producing reflected light. Clear glass absorbs little visible light, so much passes through; some reflects at its surfaces. A smooth mirror keeps reflection orderly. Rough wood scatters light; we see the table when some enters our eyes. The child lesson uses the simpler material explanation.

The basin is a programmed illustration, not experimental proof. Trials follow the chosen starting stir. In ordinary real drains, initial motion and basin shape usually dominate direction. Inward swirling can speed up, like a skater pulling their arms inward. Earth’s rotation matters at larger scales.

Ask afterwards

  1. What clue changed your idea?
  2. Which AI answer was partly right?
  3. What would you ask or check next?
